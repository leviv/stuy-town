It was summer 2021 I was a fresh graduate living in New York City for the first time. I was going out nearly every single night, exploring parks, neighborhoods, restaurants, and generally saying yes to every single opportunity that came my way. This particular night I was in East village with my roommates, eating at Spicy Moon (vegan szechuan place that has since exploded in popularity). We ordered and ate so much food that I could barely walk (it was all delicious), and I waddled out of that restaurant knowing that I needed to walk around a bit to prevent myself from immediately slipping into a food coma. I headed north up from 8th steet on 1st ave, loooking up buildings and landmarks here and there, with nothing out of the ordinary until I reached 14th street. Dismissing the architectural debates as “shadow-boxing […obscuring] the main issue,” Abrams drew attention to the project’s legal and institutional ramifications, which he saw as encouraging “social undertakings” to be “accomplished by the private sector, at a profit—with government subsidy.”109 Abrams conceded that there was an urgent need for housing, but felt that the extent of subsidy, the speed of public approval, and the lack of regulatory oversight signaled the troubling emergence of a “business welfare state” in which private interests would dictate the course of public policy"

## Reminiscences of Gilmore David Clarke

Gilmore David Clarke grew up in the Bronx mostly

"When my parents were married on November 14th, 1889, my father was in the florist business, which was the business of his father before him, my grandfather having been a gardener in the north of Ireland, where he served his apprenticeship"

"I was brought up in an environment of plants. The florist business, in those days was one where florists grew everything or a majority of things themselves and I remember that they forced things at Easter time. As a boy, I used to paint sash and get up on the greenhouses and paint and glaze. It wasn't something that I had to do; I did it because I got some spending money that way. I was influenced by the kind of work I got mixed up with, and I remember that I used to have a hot-bed with sash of my own in which I raised things. I also raised chickens and geese, and I used to take care of a horse that I had for myself. The ordinary city boy today probably wouldn't have those occupations but I did and when I went to bed at night, I was tired."

He was denied entrance to Hamilton College because he lacked a year of Latin - they required four years.

"Leaving Clinton I took the train and went as far west as Syracuse. I was traveling alone. I was then just a little over sixteen—and I knew that my mother was very apprehensive, having me travel around the state of New York by myself. I just took one look at the University upon the hill and decided that it wasn't a pleasant place and I thought that I would go on to Cornell."

He went to the College of Agriculture because tuition was free

After his first year he talked to someone in line (Edward G Lawson) at registration who was studying landscape architecture and said, "You know, I think that's a good idea" It appealed to him because his father used to decorate all the churches at easter with flowers, and gardens and backyards.

The landscape architecture department was headed by a man named Bryant Fleming. "The person who perhaps influenced me most at Cornell was Bryant Fleming."

"After graduation I returned to New York and began looking for a job." He worked at Lay and Wheelright landscape architects. Then he took a job as an engineer at the Hudson County Park Commission under Charles N. Lowrie. He used to go down through North Hudson and the other parks and see plans were carried out. Digging lakes with hands and wheelbarrows.

Then, through Scouting (he was not a scout but volunteered as a counselor) he met William White Niles and joined the Bronx Parkway Commission. He had to take a civil service exam. He first had to clean up all the lands acquired by the commission. "When the war [WWI] broke out I went to 39 Whitehall Street to see if I would be accepted for the Corps of Engineers". "Young man I admire your nerve. If a graduate of a 'farm' college seriously wants to get into the Corps of Engineers and is stubborn about it, by god I think I out to take him!"

He went to Plattsburg and was assigned to the 15th Engineer Company. He joined the Regular army, and was assigned to the 6th engineers. He went overseas in November of 1917. They constructed bridges agross the River Somme in Northern France. He stayed with the army of occupation until spring 1919. When he got back he went back to the Bronx Parkway Commission.

Most of the stone workers/craftsmen were italians. There were a lot of granite quarries in Yonkers

"I would like to focus my attention here on the Parkchester project, a project that Mr. (Leslie) Holleran, My Rapuano and I all collaborated on. while Holleran worked with us, he was not then a member of the firm and really worked under the overall direction of the Board of Design established by the Metropolitan Life Insrance Company."

"The development of Parkchester was principally envisioned by Frederick H. Ecker." It originally belonged to a Catholic orphanage. "In the early phases of the project Mr. Ecker received advice from his great friend Andrew J. Eken" He was a member of Starrett Brothers and Eken., who had built the Empire State Building. "It was through Mr. Eken that Richmond Shreve of Shreve and Lamb (who had been architects of the Empire State Building) was brought to Mr. Eckers attention".

Both Clarke and Shreve worked on the design of the New York World's fair. 

Parkchester was built with a Board of Design rather than a single architect office. Mr. Ecker was delighted with the idea and set up a special Board of Design consisting of Richmond H. Shreve as Chairman, Henry Meyer Sr. of Meyer, Strong and Oones (mechanical engineers) Andrew J. Eken of Starrett Brothers and Eken (builders) Robert W. Dowling (who represented real estate interests) and George Gove who represented the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company. Others on the Board included Irving Clavan, an architect who had been squad boss on the Empire State Building, and myself as  landscape architect.

"It was Mr. Ecker's purpose to build housing for middle income groups at the lowest rentals that could possibly be obtained in those days." Because the housing was an inestiment, "we always had in mind the factor of income."

They worked very hard to get the greatest economy of use - to cut down to the greatest possible degree the area of public space in the buildings, such as halls, and to arrange the rooms in each apartment in the most efficient possible manner. That effeciency also extended to the problem of the use of structural steel. It was found that, with careful planning, we could reduce the number of columns, thus to get the greatest economy with the least steel and still conform to the building code."That the units we devised were very efficient is evidenced by the fact that the City Housing has since adopted these basic plans many times over."

"Actually we thought of each quadrant as sufficient into itself and each area, for example, had a little group of three or four neighborhood stores. I personally never thought that this was necessary because the main stores, near the subway station, which were planned and built before the Parkchester development were not too far away from any portion of the project."

They made mocked up, real size apartments with furniture and dishes and everything for employees of Met Life to walk through and suggest improvements. This helped inform things like the best place to but light outlets, or how high people should reach for shelves in the kitchen, or what kind of kitchen arrangements. The result was they could eliminate two light outlets - with 12 or 13 thousand apartments and with light outlets at $3 apiece, you can see that we effected quite a saving.

"There were a lot of other things that we found out about merely by having people who couldn't read plans telling us what they thought would be an improvement. In a word, it paid off very well. Later when the same Board of Design was reconstituted for the planning of Stuyvesant Town, Peter Cooper Village, and Riverton, we took advantage of a lot of the criticism that had come up in Parkchester (by talking to tenants who were friendly but critical) as to how apartment details should be worked out."

"I remember that at one point we had a problem when someone got the idea that the building structures we erected weren't strong enough. The notion came to Mayor La Guardia's attention and he ordered an investigation. As I recall, inspectors came up and hoisted two pianos up to the top floor of one of the buildings and arranged to have two people play two pianos at the same time to see if the vibrations would in any way effect the structure. Once it was proved there was no danger in any of the structural work we didn't here any more about it"

Clark received the Frank P Brown medal of the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia for his role in the Parkchester development.

"Today, living in Parkchester, the outlook is better than almost any place in New York except perhaps along Riverside Drive and the borders of Central Park. Certainly there are now few apartments on Park Avenue that have the outlook that apartments in Parkchester do. In the last ten or a dozen years the trees have grown handsomely and now reach the fourth and fifth storys and as one walks through these areas now one is not conscious of the height of the buildings; one looks under the trees. It's a very attractive place to live. I don't think they have any vacancies and the rents are relatively low, in consideration of what the people get."

"Parkchester made a profound impression and a couple of years later, when the Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. started work on the housing projects in Manhattan, (Stuyvesant Town, from 14th to 20th streets, Peter Cooper Village, from 20th to 23rd and fiiverton, at 140th Street in Harlem) we were asked to join the Board of Design again. Stuyvesant Town and Riverton were constructed under the Urban Redevelopment Law."

"On the Stuyvesant Town project we benefited by our experience at Parkchester and built it as one super-block. We designed a large oval park in the middle of the project and provided for playgrounds of varying sizes in each of the four sectors. In addition we had playgrounds for teen-agers and playgrounds for little tots."

His son and his daughter in law lived in stuy-town with their kids

"In my view Stuyvesant Town has been a successful project both from an aesthetic point of view and from the operation standpoint of income."

Clarke views maintenance was what separates Stuy-town from other slums and projects.

Clarke also worked on Park Fairfax, a garden apartment develpment near Alexandria in Virginia. The govemennent was having difficulty housing people in Washinton and they approached the Metropolitan Life Insurance Co to develop several hundred acres in Arlington VA

"I suppose I could have stayed on with the Westchester County Park Commission and as a matter of fact would have if certain events hadn*t happened. One day while I was still in the hospital Mr. Moses sent an emissary to see me. This fellow told me that Mr. Mbses was going to be appointed the Park Commissioner of the City of New York by the incoming Rayor (Fiorello La Guardia) and that he wanted me to work with him. I countered, by telling this emissary—I don’t remember who it was now- that it wasn’t possible for Mr. Moses to become the Commissioner of all of the parks in the City, because each one of the five boroughs had a Commissioner. "Well, 'the man said, ’’He’s going to fix that. He's going to have the charter changed, and he's going to be the Commissioner.""

Clarkes first job was to assemble a team of "very excellend draftsmen and designers." "The work was extremely interesting and I don’t think that I ever worked harder in ray life than I did at this time." One of the first things we did was reconstruct the Central park zoo. He also tore down a number of greenhouses and built gardens in thier place. [Moses] gave me a free hand and I designed these gardens and tied them together. They are formal gardens and are known as the conservatory gardens. "The areas near the park are slums and its precisely because of this that I designed formal gardens. I knew that the people who lived close by would otherwise never get a chance to see a garden unless it was freely available in the park."

"Where land was available, and where it was adaptable to park use and would serve enough people, we put it to park use." 

He also worked on playgrounds throughout the city which informed the playgrounds in Riverton Stuytown, PCV and Rivertown.

"While talking about city planning, I said, "Well, you know, I think that what we need in this business of replanning cities is a better-informed citizenry. I don't think that the only idea is to train professional city planners that can go out and make suggestions for changes in cities. I think that it's more important to have a well-informed citizenry. In my opinion, City planning is not a profession in itself. It's a job that requires the collaboration of a lot of people—doctors in preventive medicine, the sociologist, the civil engineer, the architect, the landscape architect, the real estate operator, and the lawyer. It's a very complex piece of business."

This conversation led to him taught a course twice a week at Cornell on the history of city planning for three years. He read a ton because it was a pretty new field. When the three years were almost up he was appointed dean of the college of architecture from 1938-1950.

He was appointed a member of the national commission of fine arts by Hoover in 1932.

"I would like here to say a few words about Michael Rapuano who is still my partner today ^1952? Michael got his degree in landscape architecture from the Cornell University College of Architecture in 1927. Originally he was a resident of Syracuse and I suppose was always interested in the arts, particularly the art of the out of doors because his father was the Superintendent of Parks in the City of Syracuse. While a student he worked as a chauffer during the summers for a very distinguished member of the bar and spent a good deal of his time travelling in Italy and elsewhere in Europe. This travel gave him much valuable experience, experience that was supplemented by winning the fellowship in landscape architecture of the American Academy in Rome in 1928. He remained in Italy for two years and upon his return came to work for me in the Westchester County Park System. It was here that Michael learned about the design of parkways and expressways and participated with me in the design of the Briarcliff^ Peekskill Parkway."

"Of course he soon found that we were proceeding in this manner so as not to have to pump the water in the river in order to lay our founuatiuns anc. that it was our purpose to divert the river once we got the bridge built. The commuters who saw a bridge oeing built in the ioiddle of a field jumped to the conclusion tnat it was just another instance of Tammany graft. That was my first meeting with Bob moses. I don’t remember exactly when it occurred, but I can say that I have known him for some 40 odd years and that we have been warm friends."

"Perhaps the most trying problem in urban redevelopment is the removal of tenants from houses that are about to be torn down and finding new places for them to live.  In the case of Stuyvesant Town the -Metropolitan Life Insurance Company cooperated with this branch of the city government and in many cases found places for such dispossessed  tenants to live even though temporarily. In the beginning it was hoped that these tenants could move back to the district once Stuyvesant Town was completed. Later it was discovered that the rentals were too high to bring any of these people back and of course they had to go elsewhere. A real social problem is created when you redevelop old areas and up-root people separating them from friends, relatives and churches of their habit and choice. It wrenches at the heart strings to see some of these cases, even if one knows in his heart that in the long run the public benefit will far out weigh the individual Misery which attends such change. I don't see how one can do it any other way. There is no perfect way to do it and in no case are the people going to be entirely satisfied. I think if one can satisfy half of the people a long step has been taken."
